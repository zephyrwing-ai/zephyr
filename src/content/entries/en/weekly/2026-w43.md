## Raycast's technical refactor

[Raycast's technical deep dive](https://www.raycast.com/blog/a-technical-deep-dive-into-the-new-raycast) covers the decisions the company made while rebuilding its software and how it approached technology selection through layers of constraints. There are four layers: the Raycast shell, frontend, backend, and Rust performance layer.

The shell choice involves two UI stacks and talent considerations. The frontend involves strong operating-system integration. The backend must remain compatible with the existing JavaScript ecosystem. Rust handles performance problems, but how should its constraints be defined? There are external and internal constraints. Raycast considered the hiring market as well as its own resources, including maintenance and system state.

1. Start with the product and identify the experience users cannot do without, then choose technology that preserves or improves it.
2. Identify features that change frequently and consider how maintainability and performance affect the user experience.
3. Define boundaries for capability ownership, data flow, permissions, and security. Features such as global shortcuts, clipboard, accessibility, file-system permissions, and window management belong to the OS or native layer.

## Wise's design decision

The [discussion of Wise's design logic](https://x.com/bearliu/status/2113273422738244085) begins with why a design is the way it is. Good design requires good decisions, and understanding a design means asking what purpose it serves, why it is not designed differently, and what each difference communicates.

Wise's registration screen works as a calculator. The product's advantage is its low cost, and Wise presents that advantage through direct calculation. A calculator lets visitors calculate the result themselves.

## Technical choices across clients

The [three-part series on multi-client development](https://x.com/HiTw93/status/2112526198807183766) covers technical decisions and development experience for multiple clients. The series shows the author's engineering ability and broad development experience. It introduces Web, cross-platform, and native development, their differences, and several generations of technology.

Technology selection requires knowing the strengths and weaknesses of each option and combining that knowledge with the product scenario. Software that depends on the operating system can use native development to gain performance and the benefits of platform-level optimization. The author also discusses design systems. A personal design system can remove repeated work in later development unless the product scenario requires a new system. Finally, the series discusses IPC, or inter-process communication. A product does not have to use one language: when a problem is awkward in Swift but has a strong JavaScript implementation, Swift can call JavaScript.
